# Example thesis: Northfield Grid Systems (hypothetical)

> **Illustration only.** Northfield is a fictional company and every number below is invented to show the *reasoning*, not to describe any real company or market. It is not a recommendation. A real thesis cites a dated source for every FACT.

Condensed to the chain links that show the method; a full thesis fills all 20 sections of `templates/thesis-template.md`.

## Investment question

How structural is Northfield's advantage in large power transformers, and how much of the value created by grid investment for AI data centers can it capture over the next 7 years?

## Company profile

- **Who is this company**: Northfield builds the large transformers that step electricity down from long-distance power lines to the voltage a city district or a data center can use. Utilities and data center developers buy them because nothing reaches the building without one (FACT, would cite the annual report).
- **How the product works**: a new data center needs power → the utility must connect it to a high-voltage line → a Northfield transformer, built to order for that site, converts the line's voltage → the site gets usable power.
- **Customers**: utilities (pay and operate), data center developers (pay for their own substation; the utility often operates it). No named customer: none is disclosed (UNKNOWN).
- **How it earns**: one-off sales of custom units, paid in stages from order to delivery, plus service contracts on installed units (FACT, would cite the segment note). Whether service carries higher margins is left to the financials.
- **Where it sits**: buys steel, copper and insulation → designs and builds the unit → delivers to the utility's substation.
- **Snapshot**: makes grid transformers · sells custom units and service · utilities and data center developers pay · earns per unit plus service.

## Megatrend

```
AI adoption → compute demand → data center build-out → electricity demand
→ grid interconnection and upgrades → large power transformer demand
```

- Why now: data center power requests outpace the grid's planned additions (FACT, would cite utility interconnection queue data).
- Where value flows: to the equipment stage where capacity is slowest to add (INFERENCE, from lead-time evidence below).

## Industry

- Importance: **Critical**. No practical substitute for transformers in grid connection; outages cascade to every downstream user; governments list them as strategic (FACT, would cite government grid reports).
- Structure: top 5 makers hold about 60% of capacity in the target region (FACT, would cite industry association data; share measured by capacity, regional). Customers are utilities and data center developers, fragmented across many buyers. Utility qualification takes 2 to 4 years per design. Verdict: structure supports sustained profits for qualified incumbents (INFERENCE).

## Bottleneck

- Lead times rose from about 12 months to about 36 months in four years; makers allocate slots and take deposits (FACT, would cite industry surveys and filings).
- Constraint types: capacity, qualification, specialty steel supply.
- Northfield exposure: 55% of revenue and 70% of operating profit from large power transformers (FACT, would cite segment reporting).
- Pricing: new orders priced 30% above orders from three years ago (FACT, earnings call; Tier 2).
- Capture check: margin uplift is visible but partly offset by fixed-price legacy contracts still in backlog (INFERENCE).

## Company position and advantage

- Position: inside the bottleneck stage, with qualified designs at most large regional utilities.
- Core advantage: **qualification barrier plus installed base**. Why: utilities requalify slowly and standardise on approved designs. Evidence: win rate on tenders where it is pre-qualified is roughly double the rate where it is not (FACT, investor day). Replication: a new entrant needs a factory (3 years) plus qualification (2 to 4 years). Durability: **Medium** (3 to 7 years), because two competitors have announced new factories.

## Economic capture

```
Grid spend ↑ → transformer spend ↑ → Northfield revenue ↑ → price realisation ↑
→ gross margin 22% → 29% → FCF conversion 85% → ROIC 9% → 17%
```

- Leakage: capex for its own expansion absorbs about half of the incremental FCF (FACT, capex guidance); specialty steel suppliers raised prices 15% (FACT).
- Verdict: captures a meaningful share of the scarcity today; the share depends on pricing discipline once rival capacity arrives (INFERENCE).

## Financial evidence

- Supports: margin expansion coincides with price realisation, not volume alone; incremental ROIC above cost of capital (FACT).
- Contradicts: margins were 12% in the previous downcycle, so part of today's margin is cyclical scarcity, not moat (INFERENCE).
- Verdict: **mixed**, decided by the downcycle margin and the incremental ROIC.

## Investment debate

### What must be true

For a valuation that implies 10% revenue growth for 7 years and a 27% steady-state gross margin:

1. Lead times stay above 24 months through year 4 (INFERENCE).
2. Pre-qualification keeps Northfield's win rate above unqualified rivals' (FACT today).
3. Price realisation outruns specialty steel costs (FACT today).
4. Legacy fixed-price contracts roll off on schedule (FACT, contract disclosures).

### Exchanges

**Debate 01. Is the margin moat or scarcity?** · Lens: base rate · Under test: "Price realisation outruns specialty steel costs" (3) · Status: Leaning Bear

*Round 01 · The challenge*: does today's margin prove a moat?

- **Bull** · Testing: economic capture. Margins rose with price realisation, not volume alone, and incremental ROIC is above the cost of capital (FACT, filings). Proves: the scarcity is being captured today. Does not prove: the capture lasts.
- **Bear** · Challenging: that capture. Margins were 12% in the last downcycle (FACT). Scarcity margins in heavy equipment usually revert once capacity catches up (INFERENCE, base rate).

**Key question**: which part of 29% survives a normal lead time?

*Round 02 · The resolution*

- **Bull**: "That's a fair point." Only the pre-qualification premium is structural; the rest is cyclical. Position: reframe, from margin to qualification.
- **What changed**: Durable pricing moat: Supported → Weakened. Thesis impact: ↓ Weakens.
- **What did we learn**: Bull proved the scarcity is captured now. Bear proved most of the margin is cyclical. Unresolved: whether pre-qualification protects price (assumption 2), which Debate 02 takes up.

**Debate 02. Does pre-qualification hold when rivals add capacity?** · Lens: reflexivity · Under test: "Pre-qualification keeps Northfield's win rate above unqualified rivals'" (2) · Status: Unresolved

*Round 01 · The challenge*

- **Bear** · Attacking: pre-qualification. Three rivals announced capacity equal to about 40% of regional output, landing in the same 2-year window (FACT). Prices fall from year 3 (INFERENCE). Proves: supply is coming. Does not prove: it wins tenders.
- **Bull** · Answering that timing. Utilities qualify a new factory over 18 to 24 months, so new capacity reaches tenders late, and utilities are signing multi-year framework agreements (FACT, two disclosed). Second order: lead times ↑ → framework agreements → revenue visibility ↑ (helps).

**Key question**: do the framework agreements protect price, or only volume?

*Round 02 · The counter-evidence*

- **Bear** · Challenging: the agreements. They fix volume, not price. Evidence insufficient on their pricing terms (UNKNOWN). Thesis impact: ? Creates uncertainty.
- **Breaker found**: new orders priced below backlog for two quarters, meaning qualification no longer protects price.
- **What did we learn**: Bull proved qualification delays rivals by 18 to 24 months. Bear proved rival capacity is large and dated. Unresolved: the pricing terms of the framework agreements. If price holds, does volume?

**Debate 03. Is grid demand itself at risk?** · Under test: the demand behind assumption 1 · Status: Unresolved

*Round 01 · The challenge*

- **Bear** · Testing: demand. AI efficiency gains and on-site generation at data centers could slow grid connections (ASSUMPTION; no evidence of redesigned grids needing fewer large units).
- **Bull** · Answering that. Interconnection queues are still growing (FACT, industry association). Proves: demand is intact today. Does not prove: grid design stays the same.
- **What did we learn**: Bull proved current demand. Bear raised a risk with no evidence yet. Unresolved: grid redesign. **Frontier**: the line stops here and becomes a key unknown.

## Debate verdict

**Thesis: Unchanged · Confidence: Medium**

- **Why**: most of today's margin is cyclical scarcity (Debate 01); qualification delays rivals but the agreements' pricing terms are unknown (Debate 02); demand evidence is intact (Debate 03).
- **Weakest assumption**: 1, lead times above 24 months through year 4.
- **What would change our mind**: new orders priced below backlog for two quarters.

**What changed.** Before: Northfield holds a durable pricing moat. After: Northfield holds a qualification moat worth a structural premium; most of today's margin is cyclical scarcity that fades from year 3.
