# Investment debate

Two investors stress-test the thesis in front of an investment committee. One tries to prove it right, the other tries to break it. The aim is the test, not a winner: the thesis is forced to face the hardest questions that could break it. If it survives, it strengthens; if the attacks land, it weakens; if the evidence is missing, it becomes uncertain; if the debate finds evidence that breaks its core, it breaks. The debate is where the counter-thesis, the risks, the second-order effects, the competitive threats, the unknowns and the thesis breakers are found, so it carries all of them; the page shows no separate sections for them.

Run it after valuation, so every link of the chain, the price included, is open to attack. Build it only from the thesis's own research: every factual claim in an exchange cites evidence the run gathered, or searches for it now.

## What must be true

Before the first exchange, list the **load-bearing assumptions**: the three to seven claims the thesis falls without, ordered by how much it rests on each. Write each as a statement that can be checked ("Chip complexity keeps rising faster than design automation removes work"), with its label (FACT / INFERENCE / ASSUMPTION / UNKNOWN). This list is the debate's agenda: the Bear attacks every item on it.

## The two investors

- **The Bull** proves the thesis through structure: why the industry needs it, the bottleneck, the advantage and why it is hard to copy, how value reaches the company, long-term economics, the second-order effects that help.
- **The Bear** hunts the weak point: the assumption with the thinnest evidence, technology substitution, competitive response, supply expansion, new entrants, customer insourcing, demand slowdown, the capital cycle, commoditization, regulation and geopolitics, pricing power, whether the company really captures the value, and what the price already assumes.

Both argue to be right, never to score. Each steelmans the other, cites evidence instead of rhetoric, and keeps facts apart from inferences. Neither is a caricature: the Bull concedes, and the Bear grants strength where it is proven.

## An exchange

One exchange tests one item of What must be true. It opens with three lines:

- **Core question**: what this exchange settles, in one line.
- **Under test**: the What must be true item it attacks, quoted.
- **Status**: Open, Leaning Bull, Leaning Bear, Unresolved or Resolved, in words, never a score. Set when the exchange closes.

A **lens** (optional) sits above the core question on the few exchanges it sharpens (Investment lenses below).

### Rounds

An exchange runs in rounds, each headed by its purpose: The challenge, The rebuttal, The deeper question, The counter-evidence, The resolution. Use only the rounds the question needs: an exchange settled in two rounds ends at two.

Each round asks one question and answers it in turns. Every turn runs **claim → evidence → reasoning → implication** and opens with its **attack target**, the part of the thesis it hits: "Attacking: switching cost", "Challenging: pricing power", "Testing: share durability". Each turn takes up the turn before it: the Bear attacks what the Bull just claimed, and the Bull answers that attack. Two sides listing their own points are two essays, not a debate; rewrite until every turn answers the last one.

A round that leaves a point open ends on a **key question**, the open point in one line ("Does the growth gap come from product mix or from competitive weakness?"), and that key question is the next round's question. A round that leaves nothing open closes the exchange.

Where a round changes the thesis materially, mark its **thesis impact**: ↑ Strengthens, → Neutral, ↓ Weakens or ? Creates uncertainty. Rounds that change nothing carry no mark.

### Evidence

Evidence is tied to the claim it serves and states its reach:

- **Proves**: what the figure establishes ("Cadence grows faster in core EDA this quarter").
- **Does not prove**: the larger conclusion it cannot carry ("Cadence holds the stronger long-term moat").

Write both for every figure an argument leans on, so one number never carries a conclusion bigger than itself. Each piece of evidence carries its label: FACT for what a source states, INFERENCE for what is reasoned from it, ASSUMPTION for what the argument needs and no evidence shows, UNKNOWN for what nobody knows yet. An inference always reads as an inference.

Every argument comes from the research and has a claim, reasoning, evidence and an implication. Where the evidence is missing, the side says "Evidence insufficient", labels the claim UNKNOWN, and names what would settle it. Leave the gap visible: a filled gap reads exactly like a researched one.

### Moving positions

After a turn either side may Strengthen, Maintain, Weaken, Withdraw, Concede or Reframe, and says which. A Bull who keeps the moat but grants that pricing power is unproven reads truer than one who answers everything. When a side lands a point, the other says so ("That's a fair point."), and the concession states **what changed** as Before and After of the claim or side it moved, in Strengthened, Unchanged, Weakened or Unresolved ("Pricing power: Supported → Unresolved").

### The real question

The first question is often not the one that matters. Follow the key questions down until the exchange reaches the real investment question underneath, and show the path: initial question ("Why does Cadence grow faster?"), deeper question ("Is the gap product mix or competitive position?"), real question ("Does Synopsys capture better economics despite slower growth?").

### What did we learn

Every exchange closes on **Bull proved**, **Bear proved** and **Unresolved**, one line each, then its status. Each line names what was established ("Switching costs alone do not prove pricing power"). Summaries live here and in the verdict only.

### Adaptive order

Each exchange's core question comes from the Unresolved line of the one before. An answer about switching cost raises "does switching cost become pricing power?", whose answer raises "then why do incremental margins not expand?", whose answer opens a second-order question. A debate that alternates one pro with one con on a fixed list is a list in costume: rewrite it until each question follows from the answer before it.

### The question ladder

Climb each line of attack only as far as the next question adds material insight:

1. What is happening?
2. Why is it happening?
3. Why does it matter to the thesis?
4. Who captures the value?
5. Why this company?
6. Why can competitors not copy it?
7. What happens if conditions change?
8. What would prove us wrong?
9. What are we missing?

### Investment lenses

Name the lens as a concept, never as a quote from a named investor. Use a lens where it sharpens a question, on a few exchanges, not all of them.

| Lens | Question it asks |
| --- | --- |
| First principles | Is this advantage truly indispensable? |
| Incentives | Who is motivated to change these economics? |
| Opportunity cost | What does capital here give up elsewhere? |
| Base rate | How long do companies like this usually keep a moat? |
| Power | Who holds the bargaining power? |
| Value capture | Where does the value the industry creates end up? |
| Durability | Will what wins today still exist in five to ten years? |
| Second-order | If the company succeeds, what happens next? |
| Reflexivity | If the economics are this good, who adds supply? |
| Margin of safety | What still holds if some assumptions fail? |

### Topics

Industry importance and growth, value chain, bottleneck, position, advantage, moat and switching cost, pricing power, customer and supplier power, technology and substitution, competition, capital intensity, capture, margins and ROIC, second-order effects, valuation, management, regulation, geopolitics, AI disruption, long-term durability. Take up only the topics that can change the thesis.

## Second-order effects

When either side asks "and then what?", trace the effect in layers, each with its mechanism, evidence and label, and mark it helps or hurts the thesis:

```
First order:  AI demand ↑ → GPU utilisation ↑
Second order: → power demand ↑ → grid bottleneck ↑
Third order:  → transformer demand ↑ → lead time ↑ → pricing power ↑ → margin ↑
```

Look downstream (does the company's growth squeeze its customers, and will they push back?), upstream (suppliers' pricing and capacity), at competitors (who adds capacity, and when it arrives), at substitutes (does higher pricing make an alternative economic?) and at policy. Stop at the last layer the evidence carries, and mark it as the frontier.

## Evidence conflict

When the two sides cite evidence that disagrees, hold judgement and lay it out as a conflict: Evidence A against Evidence B, each with source, date and tier. Ask why they disagree: a different period, definition, segment, accounting treatment, source quality or interpretation. Name the data that would resolve it. A conflict that stays unresolved is labelled UNKNOWN and goes to What to watch.

## Thesis breakers

A breaker is found by the debate, never listed in advance. When a turn ends on a condition that would make a load-bearing assumption false, record it as a breaker with its indicator, threshold and source to check. The chain runs turn → breaker → KPI: the Bear's "if AI cuts verification work faster than chips grow more complex, EDA demand stalls" becomes the breaker "automation efficiency outpaces design complexity", watched through AI-assisted verification adoption and EDA workload growth.

| Breaker | Indicator | Threshold | Source to check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Market share decline | Share in core segment | Down two years running | Industry data, peer filings |
| ROIC below cost of capital | ROIC versus WACC | Below for a full year | Annual report |
| Pricing power gone | Price realisation versus input costs | Negative for two quarters | Earnings calls |
| Major customer insourcing | Top-customer capex and hiring | Announced in-house program | Customer filings |
| Superior substitute | Substitute cost and adoption | Cost parity reached | Technical documentation |
| Bottleneck relieved | Industry lead times | Back to pre-shortage level | Industry associations |

These rows are shapes, not a checklist: keep only breakers a turn actually reached, written for this company. Each one goes into What to watch.

## Search

Search for the bear case with the effort spent on the bull case: query it directly, read short reports and competitor filings, and note which supportive sources have an interest in the story. An assumption on the What must be true list that no evidence has attacked is an **unchallenged assumption**: label it so, and name the evidence that would test it.

## When to stop

Usually three to seven exchanges; more for a complex thesis. Stop when every load-bearing assumption has been attacked, the strongest counter-argument has been met, the key evidence weighed, the unknowns named and the breakers found, or when a new question stops adding material insight.

## Verdict

Close with a verdict, in words, never in points:

- **Thesis**: Strengthened (it survived the hardest questions), Unchanged, Weakened (the Bear's attacks landed), Unresolved (the evidence to decide does not exist yet) or Broken (evidence breaks a core assumption).
- **Confidence**: High, Medium or Low.
- **Why**: at most three bullets.
- **Weakest assumption**: the What must be true item the debate left thinnest.
- **What to watch**: the indicators the breakers and unknowns point at.
- **What would change our mind**: the readings that would flip the verdict.

## What changed

Then rewrite the thesis the debate leaves standing, as Before and After lines for the thesis and for each assumption it moved ("Before: Synopsys has durable pricing power. After: Synopsys has durable switching costs, but pricing power remains unproven."). This is the debate's most valuable output: it shows how the understanding moved. The After version becomes the executive thesis and the history row.
