# Investment debate

Two investors stress-test the thesis in front of an investment committee. One tries to prove it right, the other tries to break it. The aim is the test, not a winner: a thesis that survives the attack is stronger, and one that does not is revised or broken. The debate is where the counter-thesis, the risks, the second-order effects, the unknowns and the thesis breakers are found, so it carries all of them; the page shows no separate sections for them.

Run it after valuation, so every link of the chain, the price included, is open to attack. Build it only from the thesis's own research: every factual claim in an exchange cites evidence the run gathered, or searches for it now.

## What must be true

Before the first exchange, list the **load-bearing assumptions**: the three to seven claims the thesis falls without, ordered by how much it rests on each. Write each as a statement that can be checked ("Chip complexity keeps rising faster than design automation removes work"), with its label (FACT / INFERENCE / ASSUMPTION / UNKNOWN). This list is the debate's agenda: the Bear attacks every item on it.

## The two investors

- **The Bull** proves the thesis through structure: why the industry needs it, the bottleneck, the advantage and why it is hard to copy, how value reaches the company, long-term economics, the second-order effects that help.
- **The Bear** hunts the weak point: the assumption with the thinnest evidence, technology substitution, competitive response, supply expansion, new entrants, customer insourcing, demand slowdown, the capital cycle, commoditization, regulation and geopolitics, pricing power, whether the company really captures the value, and what the price already assumes.

Both argue to be right, never to score. Each steelmans the other, cites evidence instead of rhetoric, keeps facts apart from inferences, and moves when the evidence moves: when the other side lands a point, say so ("That's a fair point.") and narrow or change the argument. Neither is a caricature: the Bull concedes, and the Bear grants strength where it is proven.

## An exchange

One exchange tests one question. It holds:

- **Key question**: what this exchange settles, in one line.
- **Lens** (optional): one investment lens from the list below, shown before the question on the major exchanges only.
- **Arguments**: each side in turn, the challenged side answering. Every factual argument runs **claim → evidence → interpretation**: the claim, the evidence card it rests on (source, date, tier), and what it implies. Labels stay visible, and an inference never reads as a fact.
- **Concession**: what each side grants the other, when it grants anything.
- **Updated view**: Bull and Bear each Strengthened, Unchanged or Weakened, with the reason.
- **Implication**: what this exchange changes in the thesis.

Where evidence is missing, the side says "Evidence insufficient", the claim is labelled UNKNOWN, and the exchange names what would settle it. Leave the gap visible: a filled gap reads exactly like a researched one.

### Adaptive order

Each exchange's question comes from the weakest point of the previous answer. An answer about switching cost raises "does switching cost become pricing power?", whose answer raises "then why do incremental margins not expand?", whose answer opens a second-order question. A debate that alternates one pro with one con on a fixed list is a list in costume: rewrite it until each question follows from the answer before it.

### The question ladder

Climb each line of attack only as far as it keeps adding insight:

1. What is happening?
2. Why is it happening?
3. Why does it matter?
4. Who captures the value?
5. Why this company?
6. Why can competitors not replicate it?
7. What happens if incentives change?
8. What would prove us wrong?
9. What are we missing?
10. What must be true?

### Investment lenses

Name the lens as a concept, never as a quote from a named investor. Use a lens where it sharpens a question, on a few exchanges, not all of them.

| Lens | Question it asks |
| --- | --- |
| First principles | Is this advantage truly indispensable? |
| Incentives | Who is motivated to change these economics? |
| Opportunity cost | What does capital here give up elsewhere? |
| Base rate | How long do companies like this usually keep a moat? |
| Power | Who holds the bargaining power? |
| Value capture | Where does the value the industry creates end up? |
| Durability | Will what wins today still exist in five to ten years? |
| Second-order | If the company succeeds, what happens next? |
| Reflexivity | If the economics are this good, who adds supply? |
| Margin of safety | What still holds if some assumptions fail? |

### Topics

Industry importance and growth, value chain, bottleneck, position, advantage, moat and switching cost, pricing power, customer and supplier power, technology and substitution, competition, capital intensity, capture, margins and ROIC, second-order effects, valuation, management, regulation, geopolitics, AI disruption, long-term durability. Take up only the topics that can change the thesis.

## Second-order effects

When either side asks "and then what?", trace the effect in layers, each with its mechanism, evidence and label, and mark it helps or hurts the thesis:

```
First order:  AI demand ↑ → GPU utilisation ↑
Second order: → power demand ↑ → grid bottleneck ↑
Third order:  → transformer demand ↑ → lead time ↑ → pricing power ↑ → margin ↑
```

Look downstream (does the company's growth squeeze its customers, and will they push back?), upstream (suppliers' pricing and capacity), at competitors (who adds capacity, and when it arrives), at substitutes (does higher pricing make an alternative economic?) and at policy. Stop at the last layer the evidence carries, and mark it as the frontier.

## Evidence conflict

When the two sides cite evidence that disagrees, hold judgement and lay it out as a conflict: Evidence A against Evidence B, each with source, date and tier. Ask why they differ: a different definition, time period, scope, or source quality. Name the data that would resolve it. A conflict that stays unresolved is labelled UNKNOWN and goes to What to watch.

## Thesis breakers

A breaker is found by the debate, never listed in advance. When an exchange ends on a condition that would make a load-bearing assumption false ("automation removes design work faster than complexity adds it, for several years running"), record it as a breaker with its indicator, threshold and source to check:

| Breaker | Indicator | Threshold | Source to check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Market share decline | Share in core segment | Down two years running | Industry data, peer filings |
| ROIC below cost of capital | ROIC versus WACC | Below for a full year | Annual report |
| Pricing power gone | Price realisation versus input costs | Negative for two quarters | Earnings calls |
| Major customer insourcing | Top-customer capex and hiring | Announced in-house program | Customer filings |
| Superior substitute | Substitute cost and adoption | Cost parity reached | Technical documentation |
| Bottleneck relieved | Industry lead times | Back to pre-shortage level | Industry associations |

These rows are shapes, not a checklist: keep only breakers an exchange actually reached, written for this company. Each one goes into What to watch.

## Search

Search for the bear case with the effort spent on the bull case: query it directly, read short reports and competitor filings, and note which supportive sources have an interest in the story. An assumption on the What must be true list that no evidence has attacked is an **unchallenged assumption**: label it so, and name the evidence that would test it.

## When to stop

Usually three to seven exchanges; more for a complex thesis. Stop when every load-bearing assumption has been attacked, the strongest counter-argument has been met, the key evidence weighed, the unknowns named and the breakers found, or when a new question stops adding material insight.

## Verdict

Close with a verdict, in words, never in points:

- **Thesis status**: Strengthening, Stable, Weakening, Uncertain or Broken.
- **Confidence**: High, Medium or Low.
- **Survived because**: three to five things the Bull proved.
- **Weakened because**: three to five things the Bear proved.
- **Still uncertain**: three to five key unknowns.
- **Strongest argument** on each side, and the **weakest assumption**.
- **What to watch**: the indicators the breakers and unknowns point at.
- **What would change our mind**: the readings that would flip the status.

## Thesis update

Then rewrite the thesis the debate leaves standing, as Before and After lines for the thesis and for each assumption it moved ("Before: moat appears durable. After: moat remains strong, but AI automation is a new long-term uncertainty."). The After version becomes the executive thesis and the history row.
